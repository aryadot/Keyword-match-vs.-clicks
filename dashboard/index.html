A search relevance story

When a title just repeats your search, experts love it. People skip it.

Inside the very same search, results whose titles contain every word you typed get — fewer clicks than titles that only partly match, even though expert raters score them — points more relevant. The finding survived four attempts to break it, including a second, independent sample.

1The belief

"Put the searcher's exact words in your headline"

It's standard ad advice, and most relevance scores work the same way: the more of the query a title contains, the more relevant it looks. If that's right, the titles that copy the search should win the most clicks.

The question this project tests

If the advice is right: the more a title matches the search, the more people click, all the way to a full match.
If it's wrong: clicks stop rising, or fall, once a title just repeats the search.
2The fair test

The top spot gets clicked most, whatever it says

Before comparing titles, one trap: a result's spot on the page drives clicks on its own. So every comparison that follows asks a fairer question: did a result get more or fewer clicks than its spot alone would earn?

Share of people clicking each spot on the page

Each step down roughly halves clicks. A title at spot one would look great even if it were mediocre.

3The surprise

Experts reward the perfect match. People don't.

Expert scores keep climbing as a title shares more of the search. Clicks climb too, until the title matches every word, and then they fall below what the spot predicts.

5What it means

Looking relevant and getting clicked aren't the same thing

What I found, and why it caught my attention

  • Relevance and appeal split at exactly one point. Up to a partial match, more overlap means more clicks and higher expert scores. At a full, word-for-word match the two signals point in opposite directions.
  • Relevance itself isn't the problem. A smarter match score rose with both clicks and expert scores. What people seem to pass over is a title that only echoes what they typed, rather than one that adds something.
  • Why I find it interesting. I came to this through an interest in adtech, where headlines are routinely written to mirror the search and relevance is often judged by keyword match or human ratings. My results suggest the title that looks most relevant isn't always the one people choose, and that gap is what I'd want to understand next.

Limitations

  • Fewer clicks may not mean worse. A title that copies the question might answer it on the results page, so people don't need to click.
  • These are regular search results, not ads. Same tension, but not ad numbers.
  • Clicks and expert scores come from different searches, so the disagreement is between patterns.
  • No randomized experiment. Same-search comparisons, position adjustment and replication reduce bias but don't remove it.

What I'd test next

  • An A/B test of ad titles: full keyword match versus a partial match that adds information, measured on clicks and conversions.
  • The answer-on-the-page question: do near-copy titles hold attention longer without a click?
  • Sponsored results: repeat the analysis on ads specifically.

Data: two independent click files from Baidu's public search dataset (Baidu-ULTR) plus its expert-rated set, analyzed in DuckDB.
